📝 听力原文

Professor Chen: Good morning, Mark. Have you read that new study comparing generative AI with human creativity?

Mark: I skimmed it, Professor. The headline said AI can now beat the average human on creativity tests. That sounds almost too dramatic.

Professor Chen: Well, you're right to be skeptical. The study involved over 100,000 participants, making it one of the largest of its kind. Participants were asked to come up with alternative uses for everyday objects — a classic test known as the Alternative Uses Task. The AI systems generated responses that scored higher on average for originality.

Mark: So does that mean machines are genuinely creative now?

Professor Chen: Not so fast. What the AI did well was combining concepts from its training data in statistically unusual ways. But true human creativity involves personal experience, emotion, and intention. The study only measured one narrow dimension of creative output.

Mark: I see. So the real lesson might be that AI can assist brainstorming, but it won't replace the human creative process.

Professor Chen: Exactly. Think of it as a powerful tool for generating raw ideas, but the selection, refinement, and emotional connection still depend on us.

📝 听力原文

A new study suggests that Neanderthals did not simply disappear because of climate change or competition with Homo sapiens. Instead, researchers from the University of Oxford argue that the key difference may have been social connectivity.

By examining archaeological evidence from multiple sites across Europe, the team found that Homo sapiens formed larger and more extensive social networks. They exchanged resources, tools, and knowledge over long distances. Neanderthal communities, by contrast, were smaller and more isolated.

What's particularly striking is that these differences in social behavior may have given Homo sapiens a crucial advantage during periods of environmental stress. When resources became scarce, larger networks allowed for sharing and cooperation. Smaller groups, like those of the Neanderthals, were more vulnerable to local shortages.

In addition, the study reveals that Homo sapiens demonstrated a greater capacity for symbolic behavior, such as creating decorative objects and using pigments. These activities likely strengthened social bonds and facilitated communication across groups.

The researchers conclude that social connectivity, not just superior intelligence or technology, played a decisive role in human survival.

Octopus-Inspired Smart Hydrogel

Inspired by the remarkable shape-shifting abilities of octopuses, researchers at Penn State University have developed a smart hydrogel that can change its appearance, texture, and shape on command. This breakthrough could have wide-ranging applications in soft robotics, camouflage technology, and biomedical devices.

The material is programmed using a digital light processing technique that creates specific patterns within the hydrogel structure. When exposed to certain stimuli, such as changes in temperature or pH levels, the hydrogel responds by altering its physical properties. For example, it can transition from a smooth, transparent surface to a rough, opaque one within seconds.

However, the researchers caution that there are significant challenges ahead. One major obstacle is the material's relatively slow response speed compared to natural octopus skin. Octopus skin can change texture almost instantaneously, while the current hydrogel takes several seconds to minutes to complete its transformation.

What's more, the team found that repeated cycles of shape-changing gradually degrade the material's performance. Over time, microscopic cracks develop within the structure, reducing its ability to return to its original form. Addressing this durability issue is a priority for future research.

Despite these limitations, the hydrogel represents a significant step forward in programmable materials. The researchers envision applications ranging from soft robots that can squeeze through tight spaces to medical implants that adapt to their environment. The study also opens up new avenues for exploring how biological organisms achieve dynamic shape control.

AI Steps In as Primary Care Providers Grow Scarce

📝 核心词汇
physician /fɪˈzɪʃn/ 医师；（尤指）内科医生
shortage /ˈʃɔːtɪdʒ/ 短缺；不足
consultation /ˌkɒnsəlˈteɪʃn/ 咨询；就诊；会诊
ailment /ˈeɪlmənt/ 小病；不适
triage /ˈtriːɑːʒ/ 分诊；伤员鉴别分类
oversight /ˈəʊvəsaɪt/ 监督；监管
diagnosis /ˌdaɪəɡˈnəʊsɪs/ 诊断；判断
innovative /ˈɪnəveɪtɪv/ 创新的；革新的
priority /praɪˈɒrəti/ 优先事项；优先权
reshape /ˌriːˈʃeɪp/ 重塑；改变形态

Across the United States, a critical shortage of primary care physicians has left millions of patients without regular access to basic healthcare. In Massachusetts, the situation is particularly severe — the state's primary care workforce is shrinking faster than most other regions, forcing hospitals to search for innovative solutions.

Tammy MacDonald, a 48-year-old resident of Westwood, experienced this crisis firsthand. After her doctor passed away, she contacted ten different primary care practices near her home. None were accepting new patients. Several offices told her the wait for an appointment would be a year and a half, or even two years.

In response, Mass General Brigham, the state's largest hospital chain, launched an AI-supported program called Care Connect. Patients use a mobile app to describe their symptoms to an AI agent, which then generates a summary for a doctor. The doctor reviews the information and conducts a video consultation. The system is designed to handle common ailments such as colds, rashes, and mild mental health concerns.

But what the AI essentially does is triage — it prioritizes patient needs rather than replaces medical professionals. Without a human doctor to review and act on the AI's analysis, the system cannot function. According to healthcare experts, this human-in-the-loop model represents the most promising path forward for addressing the provider shortage.

Critics argue that the approach raises concerns about data privacy and the quality of AI-driven diagnosis. However, for patients like MacDonald, who obtained an appointment within two days through Care Connect, the benefits are hard to ignore. The story illustrates a broader trend: technology is reshaping healthcare access, but human oversight remains essential.

📖 参考范文

The rapid integration of artificial intelligence into every aspect of modern life has sparked an important debate: should we rely on AI for decision-making? In my view, while AI serves as a powerful tool for analysis and efficiency, final decisions should remain under human control. On one hand, AI offers undeniable advantages in processing vast amounts of data quickly and identifying patterns that humans might overlook. For instance, in medical diagnosis, AI systems can analyze thousands of patient records to suggest potential conditions, significantly reducing diagnostic time. However, these suggestions are only as reliable as the data they are trained on. On the other hand, human judgment brings essential qualities that AI fundamentally lacks — empathy, ethical reasoning, and contextual understanding. A doctor may override an AI's recommendation because they know a patient's personal circumstances. Therefore, the optimal approach is a collaborative model where AI provides data-driven insights while humans make the final call. In conclusion, we should embrace AI as a decision-support system rather than a decision-making authority. The future lies not in choosing between humans and machines, but in leveraging the strengths of both.
