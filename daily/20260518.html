📝 听力原文

Good morning, everyone. Today I'd like to discuss a remarkable breakthrough in brain-inspired computing. Researchers at Northwestern University have created artificial neurons that can communicate directly with living brain cells. This represents a significant step toward merging electronic devices with the human nervous system.

So, what makes this achievement different from previous attempts? In the past, artificial neurons generally produced signals that were either too slow or too simple to interact with real brain tissue. What's crucial about the Northwestern approach is that their printed neurons generate electrical signals that closely match the timing and shape of natural neural activity. When tested on slices of mouse cerebellum, these artificial signals reliably activated real neurons and triggered natural neural circuits.

However, the implications go far beyond laboratory experiments. Professor Mark Hersam, who led the study, points out that modern AI systems consume enormous amounts of energy. In fact, training large AI models requires gigawatt-scale data centers, some of which are now powered by dedicated nuclear power plants. The human brain, by contrast, performs complex tasks using only about twenty watts of power. This means the brain is roughly one hundred thousand times more energy efficient than a digital computer.

The team achieved this by using printable electronic inks made from molybdenum disulfide and graphene. These materials are deposited onto flexible polymer surfaces, creating devices that can produce a wide variety of signals, including single spikes and bursting patterns. Because each artificial neuron can generate more complex signals, fewer components are needed to perform advanced tasks.

Looking ahead, Hersam believes this technology could lead to brain-machine interfaces and neuroprosthetics that help restore hearing, vision, or movement. He also sees it as a pathway toward a new generation of energy-efficient computing hardware. While significant challenges remain, the direction is clear: the future of computing may look far more biological than silicon-based.

📝 听力原文

Today, I want to talk about a growing crisis in American healthcare and an unexpected solution. Roughly seventeen percent of adults in the United States do not have a primary care physician. This shortage is particularly severe in some states, where patients have reported waiting a year and a half or even two years for an appointment.

Why is this happening? Several factors have contributed to the problem. First, primary care doctors earn significantly less than specialists — about thirty to fifty percent less than surgeons or cardiologists. As a result, fewer medical students choose primary care as their specialty. Furthermore, the workload for primary care physicians has been increasing steadily for at least twenty years. Many describe their days as packed with complex patient visits, followed by evenings spent updating medical records.

In response to this crisis, some hospital networks have turned to artificial intelligence. Mass General Brigham, one of the largest hospital chains in the country, launched an AI-supported program called Care Connect. Here's how it works: patients download an app and spend about ten minutes chatting with an AI agent about their symptoms. The AI then sends a summary and a suggested diagnosis to a primary care doctor, who can see the patient by video, often within a day or two.

However, this approach has drawn criticism. Some experts worry that AI systems may miss important details about overlapping medical conditions. They also point out that AI cannot assess whether patients can afford follow-up care or understand their family situations, things that primary care physicians come to understand through long-term relationships.

Despite these concerns, patients like Tammy MacDonald, who was turned away by ten different practices, have found the system helpful. She describes it as a logical short-term solution. The debate over AI in healthcare is far from settled, but one thing is clear: the shortage of primary care doctors is not going away, and technology will likely play an increasingly important role in addressing it.

Artificial Neurons: Bridging Electronics and the Brain

📝 核心词汇

interface /ˈɪntərfeɪs/ n. 接口，界面；v. 对接，交互

Engineers at Northwestern University have achieved what was once considered science fiction: they have created artificial neurons that can directly interact with living brain cells. These flexible, low-cost devices produce electrical signals that closely resemble those generated by real neurons, allowing them to activate biological brain tissue in laboratory experiments.

The significance of this breakthrough lies in its potential applications. One promising direction is brain-machine interfaces — devices that could help restore hearing, vision, or movement in patients with neurological damage. By bridging the gap between electronics and the nervous system, artificial neurons could lead to a new generation of neuroprosthetic implants.

However, computing efficiency may be an even more important motivation. Professor Mark Hersam, who led the study, argues that modern AI systems consume staggering amounts of power. Training large AI models now requires gigawatt-scale data centers, some of which rely on dedicated nuclear power plants. For context, the human brain operates on roughly twenty watts — about the same as a dim light bulb. This makes the brain approximately five orders of magnitude more efficient than even the most advanced digital computers.

To replicate the brain's efficiency, Hersam's team used a novel approach. Instead of traditional silicon, they built their artificial neurons using printable electronic inks containing molybdenum disulfide and graphene. These materials were deposited onto flexible polymer surfaces using aerosol jet printing. A key insight was that the team deliberately left some polymer in place, using it to create conductive filaments that produce sudden electrical spikes, closely mirroring how real neurons fire.

When tested on slices of mouse cerebellum, the artificial signals reliably activated real neurons. The timing and duration of the electrical spikes matched natural neural activity more closely than any previous attempt. This represents a crucial step toward electronics that can truly integrate with the brain, opening the door to both medical applications and a new paradigm of energy-efficient computing inspired by nature's most remarkable machine.

Can AI Solve the Doctor Shortage?

For millions of Americans, finding a primary care doctor has become nearly impossible. Roughly seventeen percent of adults in the United States currently lack a primary care physician, and the situation is worsening. In Massachusetts, for instance, patients have reported being told they would need to wait a year and a half for an appointment, even when they have private health insurance.

The root causes of this shortage are well documented. Primary care doctors earn significantly less than specialists — on average, thirty to fifty percent less than surgeons or cardiologists. This pay gap discourages medical students from entering the field. Meanwhile, the workload for primary care physicians has increased dramatically over the past two decades, with many spending evenings completing paperwork after long days of patient care.

In response, some healthcare systems are exploring unconventional solutions. Mass General Brigham, one of the nation's largest hospital networks, launched an AI-supported program called Care Connect. Patients interact with an AI agent that collects information about their symptoms, then sends a summary to a primary care doctor who can see them by video, often within days rather than months.

Nevertheless, the use of AI in this context remains controversial. While supporters highlight its ability to reduce wait times and ease doctor burnout, critics argue that AI systems cannot fully assess a patient's circumstances. An AI tool might miss important details about overlapping health conditions, and it cannot understand whether a patient can afford follow-up care or has family support.

Despite these limitations, early users have reported positive experiences. Tammy MacDonald, who was turned away by ten different practices, described the program as a logical short-term solution. As the shortage of primary care doctors continues to grow, AI-assisted care may become an increasingly common feature of the healthcare landscape, even if it remains a imperfect substitute for the traditional doctor-patient relationship.

📖 参考范文

Artificial intelligence has become an inseparable part of modern daily life, bringing both remarkable benefits and legitimate concerns. In my view, while AI offers tremendous convenience, we must approach its integration with thoughtful caution. On the positive side, AI has made everyday tasks significantly easier. For instance, smart recommendation systems help us discover movies, music, and products tailored to our preferences. Meanwhile, AI-powered navigation apps optimize our travel routes based on real-time traffic, saving countless hours. These applications demonstrate how AI can enhance productivity and personalization in ways that were unimaginable a decade ago. However, the rapid adoption of AI also raises serious issues. A major concern is data privacy. Many AI services rely on collecting vast amounts of personal information, and users often have little control over how their data is used or shared. Furthermore, automation threatens certain jobs, as AI systems replace human workers in areas such as customer service and data entry. If left unregulated, this trend could widen economic inequality. In conclusion, AI is a powerful tool that has already improved many aspects of daily life. Yet its potential risks demand careful regulation and ethical consideration. Only by striking a balance between innovation and protection can we ensure that AI serves humanity rather than the other way around.
