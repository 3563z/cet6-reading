AI vs Human Creativity: The Largest Comparison Ever Conducted

Can generative artificial intelligence systems genuinely create original ideas? A landmark study published in Scientific Reports has sought to answer this question at an unprecedented scale, comparing the creative abilities of leading AI models — including ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini — against more than 100,000 human participants. The findings reveal a surprising and nuanced picture of where machines stand in relation to human creativity.

Researchers from the Université de Montréal, in collaboration with renowned AI pioneer Yoshua Bengio, evaluated several large language models using the Divergent Association Task (DAT), a well-established psychological test that measures divergent thinking — the ability to generate diverse and original ideas from a single prompt. Participants, whether human or AI, were asked to list ten words that are as unrelated in meaning as possible. A highly creative response might include words like "galaxy, fork, freedom, algae, harmonica, quantum, nostalgia, velvet, hurricane, photosynthesis."

The results marked a clear turning point. Some AI systems, particularly GPT-4, exceeded the average human score on these creativity measures. "This result may be surprising — even unsettling," remarked Professor Karim Jerbi, the lead author. However, the study also revealed a critical distinction: while AI outperformed the average person, the most creative humans — especially those in the top 10 percent — still maintained a clear and consistent advantage over even the strongest AI models. When researchers examined only the most creative half of human participants, their average scores surpassed every AI model tested.

The study went beyond simple word associations to explore real-world creative tasks, including composing haiku poetry, writing movie plot summaries, and producing short stories. The results followed the same pattern: AI could match or slightly exceed average human performance, but the most skilled human creators consistently delivered more original and compelling work. The researchers also discovered that AI creativity can be adjusted by changing a parameter called "temperature" — lower settings produce safer, more conventional outputs, while higher temperatures generate more varied and exploratory responses. Additionally, the way prompts were written significantly influenced AI output quality.

The researchers emphasize that these findings should not fuel fears of machines replacing human creators. Instead, they suggest that AI can serve as a tool to augment human creativity rather than replace it. The study highlights that peak creativity remains a distinctly human strength, and that human guidance — through careful prompt design and interaction — remains essential to unlocking the best from AI systems. As AI continues to evolve, understanding its relationship with human creativity becomes ever more important for shaping the future of art, science, and innovation.

Loneliness and Memory: A Surprising Link in Older Adults

Feeling lonely may affect how well older adults remember things, but it does not appear to make memory decline happen faster over time. This counterintuitive conclusion comes from a large European study published in the journal Aging & Mental Health, which followed more than 10,000 adults aged 65 to 94 across 12 European countries over a seven-year period. The findings add a new layer of complexity to our understanding of how social and emotional factors influence cognitive health in aging.

At the start of the study, participants who reported higher levels of loneliness scored lower on both immediate and delayed memory tests. They were asked to recall as many words as possible from a list of ten that was read aloud within one minute. Those in the high-loneliness group consistently performed worse than those with lower loneliness levels. However, a surprising pattern emerged over time: although they started at a disadvantage, individuals with higher loneliness did not experience faster memory decline. Their rate of decline was similar to those in the low and average loneliness groups over the seven-year period.

Loneliness has become an increasingly prominent public health concern. Previous research has linked it to a wide range of negative health outcomes, including heart disease, depression, weakened immune function, and even shorter lifespan. The relationship between loneliness and dementia risk, however, has remained controversial. Some earlier studies suggested that loneliness accelerates cognitive decline, while others found no clear connection. This large-scale study aimed to resolve some of that uncertainty by carefully tracking memory changes over time while controlling for other factors such as physical activity, social engagement, depression, and chronic health conditions.

The researchers also discovered interesting regional patterns. The highest levels of loneliness were reported in Southern European countries (12 percent), followed by Eastern Europe (9 percent), Northern Europe (9 percent), and Central Europe (6 percent). Participants in the high-loneliness group were more likely to be older, female, and in poorer overall health, with higher rates of depression, high blood pressure, and diabetes. These demographic patterns suggest that loneliness often coexists with other health challenges, making it difficult to isolate its specific effects on cognition.

The study's lead author, Dr. Luis Carlos Venegas-Sanabria, emphasized that these findings do not mean loneliness is harmless for brain health. Rather, the fact that lonely individuals start with lower memory scores is itself a significant concern. The researchers propose that routine screening for loneliness could become part of standard cognitive health assessments for older adults. By identifying and addressing loneliness early, healthcare providers may be able to support healthier aging — even if loneliness does not directly accelerate memory decline. "The study underscores the importance of addressing loneliness as a significant factor in the context of cognitive performance in older adults," Dr. Venegas-Sanabria concluded.
