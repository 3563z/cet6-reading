Quantum Leap: Stable Encryption Over 120 Kilometers Points to a Secure Future

In a significant breakthrough for quantum communication, scientists have demonstrated a remarkably stable quantum encryption system that worked across more than 120 kilometers of optical fiber. Using tiny semiconductor quantum dots that emit single particles of light — known as photons — researchers at a joint European-Chinese collaboration achieved what many experts considered a crucial milestone on the path toward an unhackable global communication network. The findings, published in the journal Nature Photonics, mark a decisive step beyond laboratory-scale demonstrations toward practical, real-world quantum communication infrastructure.

The core of the system lies in the quantum dots themselves — nanoscale semiconductor crystals that can be precisely tuned to emit individual photons on demand. Unlike conventional lasers that emit billions of photons at once, quantum dots can be engineered to release exactly one photon at a time, making them an ideal source for quantum key distribution (QKD), a method that uses the principles of quantum mechanics to secure data transmission. In conventional encryption, a sufficiently powerful computer could theoretically break any code given enough time. Quantum key distribution, by contrast, relies on a fundamental property of the quantum world: the act of measuring a quantum state inevitably disturbs it. This means that any attempt by an eavesdropper to intercept the encryption key would be instantly detectable, rendering the communication effectively impervious to interception.

However, previous attempts to use quantum dots for long-distance QKD have been plagued by stability problems. The single-photon sources were notoriously difficult to maintain over extended periods, with their performance degrading rapidly due to environmental noise and temperature fluctuations. The research team overcame this challenge by developing a sophisticated feedback-control system that continuously adjusts the quantum dot’s operating conditions, maintaining stable single-photon emission for over 24 hours. This represents a dramatic improvement over earlier systems that could only sustain stable operation for minutes at a time. The encrypted transmission achieved a record-low error rate of less than 1%, and the system successfully exchanged encryption keys at a rate of over 100,000 bits per second — fast enough for practical applications such as secure video conferencing and financial transactions.

The implications of this achievement are far-reaching. While current QKD systems based on attenuated laser pulses have been deployed in limited commercial settings — such as protecting banking networks in Switzerland and government communications in China — they suffer from fundamental limitations related to security and speed. Quantum dot-based sources offer both higher security guarantees and the potential for much faster key generation rates. The researchers estimate that with further refinement, quantum dot QKD systems could be deployed in metropolitan-scale networks within three to five years, bringing ultra-secure communication to cities. Beyond encryption, stable quantum dot sources could also serve as building blocks for emerging technologies like quantum repeaters and quantum networks, eventually forming the backbone of a future quantum internet. As one physicist involved in the project remarked, “We’ve moved beyond asking whether quantum communication is possible — now we’re asking how quickly we can build it at scale.”

Your Conversations with AI May Not Be as Private as You Think

Every day, millions of people turn to AI chatbots like ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok for help with everything from composing emails and writing code to seeking emotional support and discussing personal problems. But a revealing new study has raised troubling questions about just how private those conversations really are. Researchers at IMDEA Networks Institute in Spain have discovered that major AI platforms incorporate tracking technologies from companies including Meta, Google, and TikTok — raising the possibility that intimate details shared with an AI could be used for targeted advertising, user profiling, or data monetization in ways users never anticipated.

The study, which analyzed the network traffic of several popular AI chatbots, found that these platforms use various types of trackers — small pieces of code embedded in web pages and mobile applications that collect data about user behavior. These trackers can capture a surprising amount of information: the topics of conversation, the length of interactions, the device being used, the user’s approximate location, and in some cases even fragments of the actual prompts typed into the chatbot. What makes this particularly alarming is the discrepancy between user expectations and actual practice. In a survey conducted alongside the technical analysis, 72% of respondents said they believed their AI conversations were private, and most were unaware that their chat data could be shared with third parties for advertising and analytics purposes. As one of the study’s lead researchers explained, “Users treat these chatbots as confidential advisors — they share deeply personal information they would never post on social media — but the reality is that this data may be flowing through the same advertising infrastructure that tracks their browsing habits.”

The implications for privacy are profound. Unlike a traditional web search, where revealing a health condition or financial concern through a search query is concerning enough, a conversation with an AI chatbot often involves extended, multi-turn disclosures that build up a detailed profile. A person might tell a chatbot about their mental health struggles, workplace conflicts, relationship problems, or political views — information that, in combination with existing advertising profiles, could paint an extraordinarily intimate portrait of an individual. Privacy advocates argue that current regulations, including Europe’s GDPR and California’s CCPA, were designed for an earlier era of data collection and may not adequately address the unique challenges posed by conversational AI. Unlike a website whose privacy policy might state it uses cookies, an AI chatbot’s data practices are often far less transparent, buried in lengthy terms of service that few users read.

In response to the study, some AI companies have announced plans to enhance their privacy protections. OpenAI, for instance, has introduced an option to disable chat history and model training on user conversations, while Anthropic has emphasized its commitment to minimizing data retention. However, critics argue that these measures are insufficient, as the presence of third-party trackers operates independently of such settings. The researchers call for clearer regulatory guidelines specifically addressing AI chatbot privacy, as well as technical standards that prevent third-party tracking in AI interactions altogether. Until then, they advise users to treat their AI conversations with the same caution they would apply to any online activity involving sensitive information — a sobering reminder that even the most helpful digital assistant may not be as discreet as it appears.
