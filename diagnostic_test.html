A largely overlooked renewable energy source is gaining renewed attention from researchers and policymakers: ocean thermal energy conversion, or OTEC. The principle is elegantly simple. In tropical waters, the ocean surface can reach temperatures of twenty-five degrees Celsius, while just one thousand meters below, the water remains at a near-constant five degrees. OTEC systems exploit this temperature difference to drive a turbine and generate electricity. Unlike solar and wind power, which fluctuate with weather conditions, OTEC offers the tantalizing promise of continuous, round-the-clock baseload power. The technology was first demonstrated in 1930 by French physicist Georges Claude off the coast of Cuba, but high construction costs and low efficiency kept it in the experimental stage for decades. Now, advances in heat exchanger technology and deep-water pipeline materials are bringing OTEC closer to commercial viability. A pilot plant off the coast of Hawaii currently produces one hundred kilowatts of power, and Japan has committed significant funding to a ten-megawatt facility planned for completion by 2028. Environmental assessments suggest that OTEC's impact on marine ecosystems is minimal, though concerns remain about the discharge of nutrient-rich deep water altering local food chains. Despite these challenges, the International Renewable Energy Agency estimates that OTEC could theoretically supply over five thousand gigawatts of power — more than double current global electricity consumption.

A growing body of research is establishing a clear connection between urban green spaces and mental well-being. City planners are increasingly incorporating these findings into urban design, with Singapore's park connector network and Copenhagen's pocket parks serving as widely admired models.

🎧 讲座 1 — The Psychology of Decision Fatigue

先阅读选项，听音频后作答。音频自行播放下方脚本。

📄 点击查看听力原文（做完后对照） 
Today I want to discuss a phenomenon that affects every one of us, often without our awareness: decision fatigue. The core idea, first proposed by social psychologist Roy Baumeister, is that decision-making operates like a muscle — it becomes fatigued with use. Each choice we make, from what to eat for breakfast to which email to answer first, depletes a shared mental resource. When this resource runs low, we either make impulsive decisions or avoid deciding altogether. In a now-classic study, Baumeister and his colleagues demonstrated that participants who had to make a series of choices about consumer products later gave up far more quickly on a challenging puzzle task than those who had merely evaluated the same products without choosing. The implication is striking: the act of choosing, not just the difficulty of the options, drains our cognitive reserves. What makes decision fatigue particularly relevant in modern life is the sheer volume of choices we face. A typical adult makes an estimated thirty-five thousand decisions per day, many of them trivial but cumulatively depleting. This helps explain why judges, as studies have shown, are more likely to grant parole early in the morning than late in the afternoon — their decision-making capacity simply runs out. How can we protect ourselves? The most effective strategy is to reduce unnecessary decisions through routine and automation. When Barack Obama was president, he famously wore only gray or blue suits, explaining that he had too many important decisions to make to waste energy on what to wear. Similarly, structuring your most demanding cognitive work for the morning, before decision fatigue sets in, can substantially improve the quality of your choices. Ultimately, the science of decision fatigue teaches us that willpower is not a character trait but a biological resource — one that must be budgeted as carefully as time or money.

🎧 讲座 2 — The Archaeological Mystery of the Indus Valley Script

Among the great civilizations of the ancient world, the Indus Valley civilization stands apart for one compelling reason: its writing system remains completely undeciphered. Flourishing between 2600 and 1900 BCE across what is now Pakistan and northwest India, this Bronze Age society built meticulously planned cities with advanced drainage systems, standardized brick sizes, and a sophisticated trade network spanning from Mesopotamia to Central Asia. Yet despite over a century of scholarly effort, the meaning of the symbols inscribed on their seals, pottery, and copper tablets continues to elude us. The challenge of deciphering the Indus script is formidable for several reasons. First, we have no bilingual inscription — unlike the Rosetta Stone, which provided the key to Egyptian hieroglyphs by presenting the same text in three known languages, no comparable artifact has been found for the Indus script. Second, the surviving inscriptions are remarkably short, averaging only five symbols each, with the longest containing just twenty-six characters. This brevity makes statistical analysis extremely difficult. Third, we do not know what language the Indus people spoke, though competing theories propose connections to Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, or even a now-extinct language family. The debate extends even to whether the symbols constitute a true writing system at all. Some researchers, pointing to the limited number of unique signs and the lack of long texts, argue that the Indus symbols represent a non-linguistic system of symbols — perhaps for economic or religious record-keeping — rather than a language-encoding script. However, computational analyses using machine learning have recently identified statistical patterns in symbol sequences that resemble those found in known writing systems, offering tentative support for the linguistic interpretation. The decipherment of the Indus script, if achieved, would open an unprecedented window into one of the world's earliest urban civilizations and its ways of thinking.

🎧 讲座 3 — How Soil Microbiomes Influence Climate Change

先阅读选项，听音频后作答。音频自行播放下方脚本。

📄 点击查看听力原文（做完后对照） 
When we think about climate change, the images that typically come to mind are smokestacks, traffic jams, and melting glaciers. But beneath our feet lies a realm that may hold equal significance for the planet's climate future: the soil microbiome. A single teaspoon of healthy soil contains more microorganisms than there are humans on Earth — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and countless other species engaged in a complex web of metabolic activity. What makes the soil microbiome relevant to climate change is its role in the carbon cycle. Plants absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere through photosynthesis and transfer a significant portion of this carbon into the soil through their roots, feeding the microbial community below. In return, these microorganisms process organic matter, releasing some carbon back into the atmosphere while locking the rest into stable soil compounds that can persist for centuries or even millennia. The balance between these processes — carbon storage versus carbon release — is exquisitely sensitive to environmental conditions. What concerns soil scientists is that rising temperatures are accelerating microbial metabolism. As soils warm, microorganisms become more active, breaking down organic matter at faster rates and releasing greater quantities of carbon dioxide and methane into the atmosphere. This creates a potentially devastating feedback loop: warming triggers microbial carbon release, which intensifies warming, which triggers further release. Some models suggest that this soil carbon feedback alone could add half a degree Celsius to global temperatures by the end of the century. However, the news is not entirely bleak. Research has shown that certain agricultural practices can tip the balance toward carbon storage. No-till farming, cover cropping, and the application of compost not only reduce emissions from soil but actively draw carbon down from the atmosphere — a process known as carbon sequestration. A team at the University of California, Berkeley, demonstrated that applying a single layer of compost to rangelands increased soil carbon storage by one ton per hectare annually over a decade, while simultaneously boosting forage production. The emerging message is clear: the soil beneath our feet is not merely a passive substrate but an active, dynamic player in the climate system — one that we ignore at our peril.

📖 Section C — 仔细阅读：Why Boredom Is Essential for Creativity

In an age of constant connectivity, where a smartphone offers an escape from every idle moment, genuine boredom has become an increasingly rare experience. The average person now reaches for their phone within seconds of finding themselves without a specific task, filling what researchers call "micro-waiting periods" — the thirty seconds in an elevator, the two minutes waiting for coffee. This behavior, while seemingly harmless, may carry a hidden cost: the gradual erosion of our capacity for creative thought.

Psychologists have long distinguished between two types of boredom. The first, situational boredom, is the familiar restlessness that arises during a dull meeting or a long queue. The second, existential boredom, is a deeper sense of disengagement from one's activities or life direction. While situational boredom is merely uncomfortable, existential boredom can serve as a powerful signal that one's current path lacks meaning. Both forms, however, share a crucial feature: they create mental space. When external stimulation is absent, the brain defaults to what neuroscientists call the default mode network — a set of brain regions that becomes active during rest and is strongly associated with creative thinking, autobiographical memory, and future planning.

Research supports the creative benefits of allowing the mind to wander. In a 2014 study published in the Creativity Research Journal, participants who completed a deliberately boring task — copying numbers from a phone book — before attempting a creative challenge generated 41 percent more ideas than those who went straight into the challenge. Another study asked participants to generate as many uses as possible for a pair of plastic cups; those who had first endured a period of boredom outperformed both a control group and a group that had been given a restful but non-boring activity.

Yet the modern digital environment has systematically eliminated the conditions that make such mind-wandering possible. Streaming services provide endless entertainment on demand. Social media feeds are engineered to be infinitely scrollable. Podcasts and audiobooks fill every commute and household chore. The result is what some psychologists term "continuous partial attention" — a state in which the mind never fully rests but never fully engages either. This state occupies the cognitive middle ground where neither focused work nor creative incubation can occur.

How can individuals reclaim the productive potential of boredom? The answer is not to seek out unpleasant experiences, but to deliberately create "unfilled time" — periods without digital input during which the mind is free to drift. Taking a walk without a phone or podcast, eating lunch away from screens, or simply sitting with a cup of tea and no agenda are all practical starting points. The goal is not to become bored in the negative sense, but to restore the mental breathing room that creativity requires. In a world that increasingly equates constant activity with productivity, the counterintuitive truth is that doing nothing may be one of the most productive things we can do.

📖 Section C — 仔细阅读：The Decline of Third Places and Its Social Cost

In his influential 1989 book The Great Good Place, sociologist Ray Oldenburg introduced a concept that has since become central to urban studies: the "third place." Distinct from the first place of home and the second place of work, third places are informal public gathering spots — cafes, barbershops, community centers, public libraries — where people from different backgrounds meet on neutral ground, conversation is the primary activity, and the atmosphere is welcoming and unpretentious. Oldenburg argued that these spaces are not luxuries but necessities for democratic society, serving as the informal infrastructure through which community bonds are formed and civic life is sustained.

Three decades later, third places are in decline across much of the developed world, and the consequences are becoming increasingly visible. The causes are multiple and interconnected. Rising commercial rents in urban areas have forced independent cafes and bookstores to close, replaced by chain establishments that, while physically similar, lack the social warmth and tolerance for lingering that characterize genuine third places. The shift to remote work has emptied downtown areas, reducing the foot traffic that once sustained neighborhood gathering spots. And digital communication, for all its conveniences, has partially displaced the need for physical meeting places — though evidence suggests it has not replaced the depth of connection they provide.

The erosion of third places carries measurable social costs. A 2023 American Enterprise Institute study found that the number of Americans who report having zero close friends has quadrupled since 1990, rising from 3 percent to 12 percent. While multiple factors contribute to this trend, researchers consistently point to the decline of spontaneous, low-stakes social environments as a significant driver. Unlike organized social activities — clubs, classes, scheduled gatherings — third places allow relationships to develop organically, without the pressure of formal participation. The retiree who becomes a regular at the local diner, the graduate student who strikes up conversations at the neighborhood pub, the parent who bonds with other parents at the playground — these connections form not through intention but through repeated, unplanned proximity.

Some communities are fighting back. In Portland, Oregon, the city government has established a "Third Place Preservation Fund" that provides grants to independently owned gathering spaces at risk of closure. In Japan, the concept of "ibasho" — a place where one feels a sense of belonging — has been integrated into urban planning policy, with municipalities required to ensure that every neighborhood has accessible community spaces within walking distance. These efforts recognize something that Oldenburg understood decades ago: that the health of a society can be measured not just by its economic output but by the quality and accessibility of the spaces where its citizens gather informally, without agenda, simply to be among one another.
